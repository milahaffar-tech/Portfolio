Room I · On going deeper · Hospitality

MOF

A positioning territory for a new ultra-luxury Turkish hotel group: go deeper, not bigger.

From my notebook

Every resort in Antalya competes by adding more: bigger waterparks, longer buffets, another golf course. Whatever gets added gets copied. I kept asking what would happen if a hotel went deeper instead.

Role
Brand strategy
Context
MOF Hospitality, Türkiye
Output
Positioning territory and brand vision
Skills
Cultural research, audience insight, brand positioning

The brief

MOF wanted a new ultra-luxury hospitality brand that moves Turkish resort hospitality away from scale, standardization, and the all-inclusive formula, toward something more private, more emotionally resonant, and credible on a global stage as Mediterranean luxury living.

The work covered the strategic, verbal, and visual foundations for an upcoming resort, plus a brand vision clear enough to set the ambition for everything after it.

What I did

I developed one of three positioning territories the brand could take: Turkish Culture, Woven In. It rests on three foundations.

01

Background

  • The family behind the brand started as a textile company, Ozak Textile, founded in 1985.
  • Turkish craft and heritage were in their DNA before they ever opened a hotel.
  • That gives them more permission than any other hotel group to champion Turkish culture.
02

Audience insight

  • Luxury travelers want authentic, local experiences, not tourist attractions.
  • The new luxury isn't “more to do.” It's more to connect with.
  • Turkey ranks second only to China for living cultural heritage on UNESCO's list, so the material is already there.
03

Categorization

  • Antalya is crowded with big, all-inclusive, family-focused resorts.
  • Hotels compete by adding more: bigger waterparks, 24-hour dining, golf courses.
  • Those amenities get copied fast, so every hotel ends up looking the same.

My story in short: Turkish DNA is woven into this brand, literally. Weaving and textiles run deep in Turkish culture, and the client's own history started there. That heritage carried through into construction, their first resort, and now Saphira. The brand doesn't need to invent a cultural story. It already has one to build on.

The solution

I proposed positioning Saphira as the considered luxury Turkish hotel group: going deeper, not bigger.

  • Culture as the experience. Rituals, craft, and living traditions, like Turkish coffee reading, replace generic resort entertainment.
  • Hospitality rooted in Tanrı Misafiri. Each guest is welcomed as a blessing, not a booking. That warmth sets it apart from Swiss precision or American efficiency.
  • A brand that travels. Turkish roots keep Saphira distinct as it grows into the wider Mediterranean, where everyone else reaches for the same sun, sea, and olive-branch imagery.

The promise: rooting guests to this land, not just housing them in it.
